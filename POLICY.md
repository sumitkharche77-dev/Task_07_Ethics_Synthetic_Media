# Synthetic Media Policy
## Mohawk Valley Courier — a local/regional news outlet

**Effective:** October 1, 2026 · **Owner:** Managing Editor · **Applies to:** all
staff, freelancers, and contractors producing content for broadcast,
digital, or social publication under this organization's name.

---

## 0. Why This Policy Exists

This organization's product is not footage or audio — it is audience trust
that when a real voice speaks under our name, a real person said those
words and stands behind them. Synthetic media tools now make it possible
to produce narration, translated segments, or fully simulated interview
content at a cost and speed that did not exist five years ago. Those same
properties — cheap, fast, indistinguishable from the real thing to a
casual listener — are what make misuse of this technology so damaging to
an organization whose core asset is exactly the trust these tools can
counterfeit.

This policy governs when synthetic media may be used in our work, how,
and under what safeguards. It exists because "use AI responsibly" is not
a policy — a policy is a document specific enough that a producer on
deadline can check a proposed use against it and get a clear answer.

## 1. Permitted Uses

Synthetic voice narration is permitted, without special review, for:

- **Non-attributed explanatory narration** reading content this newsroom
  has independently verified — for example, a generic synthetic voice
  narrating a data-driven explainer, structurally similar to the analysis
  narration this organization has previously produced in R&D testing.
- **Translated or localized versions** of already-published stories,
  where the synthetic voice reads a translation of our own verified text
  and is clearly identified as a translation/narration, not as the
  original reporter or subject speaking.
- **Internal drafts and rough cuts** never published externally — using
  synthetic narration to time a script or preview an edit before a real
  voiceover is recorded.
- **Accessibility narration** — e.g., synthesized audio description of
  visual content for accessibility purposes, clearly identified as such.

The common thread across all permitted uses: **no real, identifiable
staff voice or likeness is cloned or simulated, and no synthetic content
is formatted as an interview, quote, or statement attributed to a real
person.** A producer can check any proposed use against this line: if it
would let a viewer reasonably believe a specific real person said
something they did not actually say, it is not a permitted use — see
Section 2 or Section 6.

## 2. Prohibited Uses

The following are refused outright, regardless of intent, disclosure, or
labeling:

1. **Cloning or synthesizing the voice or likeness of any real,
   identifiable person — staff, source, subject, or public figure —
   without their documented, current, specific consent** (see Section 3).
   This applies even for content that is true, flattering, or intended
   humorously.
2. **Any synthetic content formatted as an interview, quote, statement, or
   Q&A exchange attributed to a real person who did not actually say
   those words**, even if the underlying facts referenced are accurate.
   Borrowing the *structure* of an interview to deliver information a
   real person never actually said is prohibited independent of whether
   the content itself is true (see ETHICAL_ANALYSIS.md, Truth axis and
   Format Authority axis).
3. **Any synthetic political-figure content during an active campaign
   period** (from the opening of candidate filing through certification
   of results in the relevant jurisdiction), regardless of disclosure or
   purpose. This newsroom does not produce synthetic media of candidates
   or ballot measures during this window, full stop.
4. **Any synthetic content where the disclosure requirement in Section 4
   cannot be technically guaranteed to reach the audience** — for
   example, a synthetic voice track intended for a platform or format
   that strips embedded labels or metadata and provides no alternative
   disclosure mechanism.

A policy that permits everything with sufficient labeling is not a
policy. These four categories are refused independent of disclosure.

## 3. Consent Workflow

Any use of a real staff member's, source's, or subject's voice or
likeness — even for a low-stakes use such as an automated caption-reading
narration — requires:

- **Written, specific consent** describing exactly what will be
  synthesized, for what purpose, and for how long it remains valid.
  Blanket or open-ended consent is not accepted.
- **Obtained and logged by:** the Managing Editor or their designated
  delegate, recorded in a central Synthetic Media Consent Register
  (name, date, scope, expiration, and a copy of the signed consent).
- **Validity period:** consent is valid for the specific project
  described, or for a maximum of 12 months for ongoing/recurring uses,
  whichever is shorter. It does not carry forward to new projects.
- **Revocation:** the person may revoke consent at any time, in writing
  or verbally to the Managing Editor (verbal revocation is logged in
  writing within 24 hours by the person who received it). On revocation,
  production of new material using that consent stops immediately, and
  the organization removes or re-produces any *already-published* content
  covered by the revoked consent within 5 business days where technically
  feasible, and otherwise discloses the revocation alongside the
  existing content.
- **No consent obtained via the synthesis tool's own workflow counts as
  organizational consent.** A vendor's "upload a sample to clone a voice"
  step (see ETHICAL_ANALYSIS.md, Consent axis) is not a substitute for
  this newsroom's own documented consent process — the two are entirely
  separate, and staff must not treat a tool's willingness to generate a
  clone as permission to do so.

## 4. Disclosure Standards

Every synthetic-media asset this organization publishes carries:

- **A spoken or on-screen disclosure at first publication**, placed
  before the synthetic content begins (not buried in credits or
  description text), stating clearly that the voice or content is
  AI-generated.
- **A persistent label embedded in the file or its metadata** wherever
  the production tool supports it (filename convention:
  `_SYNTHETIC` suffix, consistent with this organization's Task 6
  labeling practice, plus any embedded metadata the tool provides).
- **Acknowledged limitation:** per ETHICAL_ANALYSIS.md (Context axis),
  neither of the above is guaranteed to survive downstream re-encoding,
  screen recording, or third-party re-upload. This policy does not claim
  disclosure is unstrippable — see Section 9.
- **Re-share responsibility:** any staff member clipping, excerpting, or
  re-sharing newsroom-produced synthetic content to another platform
  (including our own social accounts) is responsible for re-adding a
  disclosure to that excerpt if the original disclosure would not carry
  over automatically. A clip that drops the opening disclosure is
  treated as a policy violation by whoever published the clip, not an
  acceptable side effect of editing.

## 5. Provenance Requirements

- **Vendor watermarking enabled** on every synthesis tool used, wherever
  the tool offers it. Per our own testing (Task 6 DETECTION.md), at
  least one commercial vendor's watermark survives cutting, splicing,
  normalization, and re-export — this is a real, if partial, protection
  and is required wherever available.
- **Internal production log** for every synthetic asset: tool and
  version, date, purpose, and the staff member responsible, retained for
  a minimum of 3 years.
- **C2PA / content credentials attached where the production tool
  supports them.** This organization has not independently verified that
  content credentials survive our typical distribution pipeline
  (broadcast re-encode, social re-upload); attaching them is required as
  a best-effort layer, not represented to staff or audience as a
  guarantee.

## 6. Review and Approval

- Any proposed synthetic-media use **outside** the pre-approved
  categories in Section 1 requires sign-off from the Managing Editor (or,
  for uses touching political content, joint sign-off from the Managing
  Editor and the Standards Editor) **before production begins**.
- **Review standard applied:** would this content risk being mistaken by
  a reasonable viewer for an unsynthesized statement by a real person,
  and does synthetic media serve a purpose that a non-synthetic
  alternative (a real recorded voiceover, a written explainer) could not
  achieve at comparable cost and speed? A proposal that fails either
  question is denied or sent back for a non-synthetic alternative.
- **Turnaround commitment:** review decisions are returned within one
  business day for routine requests, or within 4 hours for
  breaking-news-adjacent requests flagged as time-sensitive. A review
  process staff cannot get a timely answer from will be routed around,
  so this commitment is binding on the reviewer.

## 7. Incident Response

If a synthetic asset produced under this policy is misattributed,
stripped of disclosure downstream, or reaches an audience in a misleading
form — whether or not this organization caused the misrepresentation:

1. **Within 24 hours of discovery:** the Managing Editor is notified and
   the relevant production log entry is reviewed.
2. **Within 48 hours:** a public correction is published on the same
   platform(s) where the misleading version circulated, clearly
   identifying what was altered or misrepresented.
3. **Internal review:** every incident is logged and reviewed at the next
   standing editorial meeting, regardless of severity, to determine
   whether Section 1–6 boundaries need revision.
4. **External reporting:** if the incident involves a third party
   maliciously stripping disclosure or impersonating this organization
   (rather than an internal process failure), the Managing Editor
   evaluates whether platform reporting or legal counsel should be
   engaged, on a case-by-case basis.

## 8. Refusal

This organization refuses to produce synthetic media at all — even for a
use that would otherwise be permitted under Section 1 — whenever a
non-synthetic alternative would serve the same editorial purpose with
materially lower misrepresentation risk, for only marginally more cost or
turnaround time. Concretely: if a story can be told with a real recorded
voiceover on the newsroom's normal production timeline, that is the
default. Synthetic narration is a tool of last resort for a narrow set of
uses (translation, accessibility, internal drafts) — not a standing
production shortcut. Any staff member may raise a refusal on these
grounds, and a refusal does not require Managing Editor sign-off; only an
*exception* to Section 2's prohibited-use list would (and no one below
the Managing Editor may grant one).

## 9. Limitations — What This Policy Cannot Guarantee

This section is not an apology for the policy. It is an account of where
it will fail, so staff can be vigilant rather than complacent about what
it covers.

**Where a well-intentioned employee could reasonably misjudge a
boundary.** The Section 2 prohibition on "content formatted as an
interview... attributed to a real person" is the boundary most likely to
be misjudged in good faith. A producer building an explainer segment
might drift from plain narration ("here is what the data shows") into
something that *feels* like an attributed quote without a clear
attribution statement ever being written down — for example, narrating
"as our source told us" over synthetic audio when the underlying
quote was actually paraphrased from a written statement, not an actual
recorded interview. This policy relies on the Section 6 review process to
catch that drift before publication, but a producer working under
deadline pressure without submitting for review is the realistic failure
mode, not a producer intentionally violating Section 2.

**What a bad-faith actor outside this organization can do that this
policy cannot stop.** This policy governs *production* by this
organization's staff and contractors. It has no mechanism to prevent a
third party from cloning a staff reporter's voice independently (see
ETHICAL_ANALYSIS.md, Consent axis), stripping disclosure from
newsroom-published content and re-distributing it (Context axis), or
fabricating content that merely imitates this organization's visual or
audio style without using any of our actual production tools or staff.
Sections 4, 5, and 7 give this organization tools to *respond* to such
misuse — correction, reporting, escalation — but nothing in this policy
prevents it from happening in the first place. That protection, to the
extent it exists at all, lives outside this document, in platform policy,
detection tooling, and law (see ETHICAL_ANALYSIS.md, Mitigation
Landscape).

**Where this policy relies on good faith, and whether that is fatal.**
The consent workflow (Section 3) relies on staff actually reporting
unauthorized attempts to use their likeness rather than staying silent
out of conflict-avoidance or uncertainty about whether an attempt even
occurred. The re-share responsibility (Section 4) relies on a staff
member clipping content actually remembering to re-add disclosure, with
no automated enforcement mechanism specified. Neither of these reliances
is unique to this policy — no policy governing human behavior can
eliminate reliance on people acting in good faith — but naming them
explicitly here, rather than implying Sections 3 and 4 "solve" consent
and disclosure, is the honest position. A policy that claimed to
guarantee outcomes it cannot guarantee would produce complacency; naming
the reliance is meant to produce vigilance instead.

**What this policy does not address at all.** It does not specify a
technical mechanism for verifying that a piece of *incoming* content
(footage or audio a source or the public sends us) is itself synthetic
before this organization treats it as a real recording — that is a
detection and verification problem, not a production policy problem, and
is out of scope for this document. It also does not specify consequences
for staff violations (a disciplinary matter for HR policy, not editorial
policy) beyond the incident-response steps above.
